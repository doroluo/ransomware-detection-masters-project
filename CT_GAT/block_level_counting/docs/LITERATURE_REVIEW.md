# Sources back the layer, not the wiring

Every layer-level choice in our hierarchical block-CFG plus call-graph classifier has a published source, but almost all of those sources were measured on molecules, citation graphs, social graphs or binary-similarity tasks, and none tested a typed, directed message-passing network on Windows PE ransomware under a family-disjoint split. The sources that come closest predicted the result we got: a structure-free model on the same tokens ties or beats a CFG model in distribution ([Ma et al., FSE 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.15552); [arXiv 2609.24280](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24280v1); [Errica et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09893)), and under our pre-registered 3-seed by 5-fold test, with 15% block masking on both arms, the typed CFG beat its no-edge twin by only **+0.004 AUC [-0.001, +0.010]**, so the rule failed (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`). Three of our choices go beyond their sources and should be stated as such in any write-up: typed CFG edges (no malware GNN types edges; the precedent is ProGraML on LLVM-IR), the sum/sqrt(n) readout term (no published graph-readout precedent), and excluding register names and operand widths (PalmTree, jTrans and SAFE all keep registers). Two literature predictions failed to replicate here: DropEdge and edge flipping, which GraphCL and AIA said should hurt, raised AUC by about +0.02, and an 8-layer CFG stack did not oversmooth. The evaluation design is where our work is strongest and most novel: no PE malware paper we found combines zero-shot family holdout, a degree-preserving shuffle control, class-by-architecture reweighting, a family-clustered paired bootstrap and a pre-registered effect size, and that combination is what stopped a seed-0 structure signal from becoming a claim. Several choices are still untested on our data: the gated readout, the depth sweep, the call-graph level on its own, learned subword tokenizers and frozen pretrained encoders.

*Conventions.* "Team" numbers come from `docs/PLAN_GNN.md` in the `rdmp-gt-blc` worktree (`CT_GAT/block_level_counting/`). Unless stated otherwise they are pooled out-of-fold over the 5 family-disjoint x86 folds (1,810 binaries in 712 clusters), seed 0, with the AUC-selected checkpoint and macro-F1 at the validation threshold. The rungs are R0 TF-IDF logistic regression, R2 hierarchy with no edges, R4 degree-preserving shuffled edges, R5 real untyped edges and R7 the full typed, directed model. "What we did" is taken from the code (`pipeline/s06_gnn_block_cfg/layers.py`, `pipeline/s07_pool_blocks_to_functions/readout.py`) where it differs from the plan's prose.

## Four layer choices rest on proofs and benchmarks from other graph domains

The layer is `h <- h + MLP(LayerNorm((1+eps) h + W [m_in ; m_out]))`, with `m_in = sum over in-edges of ReLU(h_src + E_in[type])` and `m_out = sum over out-edges of ReLU(h_dst + E_out[type])`. It runs for 3 layers over each function's block CFG with 12 edge types, and 2 layers over the call graph with one type. Each of its four ingredients has a clean source, and each source was measured somewhere other than program graphs.

### GIN sum aggregation with a (1+eps) self-term

*What we did.* Neighbour messages are summed (not averaged), and the node's own state enters as `(1 + eps) h`, with eps a learnable scalar initialised to 0. Only the final layer feeds the readout.

*Source and what it showed.* Xu et al. prove that an aggregation GNN is at most as powerful as the 1-WL test, and reaches it when neighbour aggregation and readout are injective on multisets. They show sum can be injective while mean and max cannot, and that `(1+eps) f(c) + sum f(x)` separates the centre node from its neighbours for infinitely many eps ([Xu et al., ICLR 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)). Empirically the gap is small except on featureless graphs. On REDDIT-BINARY, **GIN-0 scored 92.4 while mean aggregators collapsed to 50.0**, but on MUTAG, PROTEINS and NCI1 sum and mean sat within about a standard deviation, and the WL subtree kernel beat every GNN on NCI1 (86.0 vs 82.7) ([Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)). Under a clean model-selection protocol GIN fell to 80.0 on NCI1 and 73.3 on PROTEINS ([Errica et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09893)).

*Where we depart.* There are three departures. First, Xu et al. found **GIN-0 (eps fixed at 0) "slightly but consistently" better than GIN-eps** on test accuracy, and we train eps. Second, every injectivity result assumes a countable feature space, and the authors leave continuous features to future work. Our block vectors are continuous TF-IDF projections, and PNA proves that with continuous features no single aggregator is injective ([Corso et al., NeurIPS 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05718)). The WL argument is therefore motivation for sum, not a guarantee. Third, GIN's readout concatenates a readout of every layer (Jumping Knowledge style, Eq. 4.2), and so does the closest PE CFG model, NDSS 2025's 3-layer GIN-0 ([Li et al., NDSS 2025](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2025-830-paper.pdf)). We read out only the last layer.

*Our evidence.* None of the three departures has been ablated. The layer as a whole matched TF-IDF only after regularisation (below), which says nothing about sum against mean.

### GINE-style edge-type embeddings

*What we did.* A learned embedding of the edge type is added to the sender's state, and a ReLU is applied per edge before summation. Each layer has its own `E_in` and `E_out` tables. The 12 types are edge kind (fall-through, conditional taken, jump, indirect, extern, other) crossed with a DFS back-edge bit.

*Source and what it showed.* The per-edge form `ReLU(x_j + e_ji)` with a `(1+eps)` self-term is PyTorch Geometric's `GINEConv` ([PyG docs](https://pytorch-geometric.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/torch_geometric.nn.conv.GINEConv.html)), which cites Hu et al. Hu et al.'s own equation adds layer-specific categorical edge embeddings to the neighbour sum without a per-edge ReLU, and uses a self-loop edge category instead of eps. With that layer, pre-training gained **up to 9.4 ROC-AUC points**, but the paper does not isolate the edge embedding's contribution ([Hu et al., ICLR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12265)). The program-graph evidence for typed edges is indirect. On VarMisuse, keeping only syntax edges cost Allamanis et al.'s GGNN **30.2 accuracy points (85.5 to 55.3)** ([Allamanis et al., ICLR 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00740)). ProGraML's POJ-104 ablation raised test error from 3.38% to 7.76% (+129.6%) without data edges, to 4.16% (+23.1%) without backward edges, and to 3.88% (+14.8%) without control edges ([Cummins et al., ICML 2021](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/cummins21a/cummins21a.pdf)).

*Where we depart.* The per-layer tables match Hu et al.; the per-edge ReLU follows PyG, not Hu. That ReLU matters for us: without it, `sum (h_u + e)` splits into `sum h_u + sum e`, and the pairing between a neighbour and its edge type is lost. No primary source ablates it. The larger departure is domain. **No PE malware GNN we found types its edges.** MalGraph, MAGIC, Hi-GNN, NDSS 2025 and 2609.24280 all use one relation per level ([MalGraph](https://nesa.zju.edu.cn/download/lx_pdf_malgraph.pdf); [MAGIC](https://www.cs.binghamton.edu/~ghyan/papers/dsn19.pdf); [HiGraph](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.02113)). The typed-edge precedent comes from LLVM-IR and C#, where the strongest effects came from data-flow edges that a basic-block CFG does not have. Our kind labels are also mostly readable from the source block's last instruction, which the block encoder already sees.

*Our evidence.* At seed 0, typed beat untyped real edges by **+0.013 AUC [+0.003, +0.026] under F1 checkpoint selection**, but by +0.019 [-0.033, +0.106] under AUC selection, so the interval excludes zero under one rule only. Real untyped edges (R5, 0.945 AUC) also scored below shuffled edges (R4, 0.975). Types carry some signal, but typed edges never beat dropping the edges altogether (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1). The rungs that would isolate types, R6 (types permuted) and R8 (stacking), were never run.

### Dir-GNN-style separate in/out aggregation

*What we did.* In-edges and out-edges are summed separately, each with its own type table, and mixed by one `Linear(2d -> d)` over `[m_in ; m_out]`. The "undirected" ablation feeds `m_in + m_out`, both built from the `E_in` table, into both halves.

*Source and what it showed.* Dir-GNN proves that separate injective in- and out-aggregators match the directed WL test and are strictly more expressive than an MPNN on either the undirected or the forward-only graph ([Rossi et al., LoG 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10498)). The gains are large on heterophilic node classification and absent on homophilic data. **Roman-empire GCN rose from 56.23 to 74.54**, and Squirrel from 62.71 to 74.43, while ogbn-arxiv fell slightly, from 68.39 to 66.66 ([Rossi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10498)). On homophilic graphs the authors say Dir-GNN "has to learn to align" the two directions because their weights are independent.

*Where we depart.* `W [m_in ; m_out]` is Dir-GCN's two weight matrices without the convex weight alpha, which a linear combiner makes redundant. The departure that matters is task: all of Dir-GNN's evidence is node-level, and the authors state that they did not test other functional forms. Nothing in the paper predicts a gain for graph-level classification.

*Our evidence.* Seed-0 direction arms were **backward-only +0.016 [+0.000, +0.040], forward-only -0.034 [-0.105, +0.005] and undirected -0.037 [-0.129, +0.013]** against R7, with macro-F1 often moving against AUC. The plan reads this as checkpoint-selection noise, not a directional effect (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1b). The choice is defensible on expressiveness and unsettled on our data.

### Residual connection and pre-LayerNorm

*What we did.* We use a pre-norm residual: normalise, transform with a 2-layer GELU MLP, and add back to `h`. LayerNorm is used, not BatchNorm.

*Source and what it showed.* DeepGCNs trained a 56-layer GCN with residual connections, and removing them at 28 layers cost **12% mIoU** ([Li et al., DeepGCNs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.03751)). DeeperGCN introduced the pre-activation order (normalisation, then ReLU, then graph convolution, then addition) with BatchNorm or LayerNorm. At 112 layers it reached **0.858 ROC-AUC on ogbn-proteins, against 0.824 for a plain GCN**. At 3 layers residuals gave nothing: 0.834 plain, 0.824 ResGCN, 0.829 ResGCN+ ([Li et al., DeeperGCN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07739)). DropEdge's tables show the same pattern on Cora at 32 layers, **71.60 for GCN against 85.10 for ResGCN** ([Rong et al., ICLR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10903)).

*Where we depart.* The published benefit is for 28 to 112 layers, and at our 3 layers the sources predict no accuracy gain. Choosing LayerNorm over BatchNorm is a design argument, not a cited result: per-node statistics do not depend on batch composition, which swings from a few hundred blocks to 235k. LayerNorm also rescales unnormalised sums from high-degree blocks, which is the job PNA's log-degree scaler does ([Corso et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05718)).

*Our evidence.* There is no direct ablation. The 8-layer arm trained stably and scored 0.978 AUC against 0.964 for 3 layers (+0.014 [-0.004, +0.041]), which is consistent with residuals doing their job at depth (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1b).

## Message passing replaced the transformer on cost grounds, at a depth inside the published range

### Message passing over Graphormer/GPS

*What we did.* The previous model was a hierarchical graph transformer: 2 pre-norm layers, 4 heads, per-head shortest-path-distance attention biases clipped to {0, 1, 2, 3, 4+, unreachable}, and in/out-degree embeddings, over address-ordered segments of at most 128 blocks (team, `docs/PLAN.md`). We replaced it with the typed MPNN above, keeping every block, and gave every arm the same 2-layer MLP head.

*Source and what it showed.* Graphormer adds shortest-path biases, degree centrality and path edge encodings to full attention. It won PCQM4M-LSC with **0.1234 validation MAE against 0.1395 for GIN-VN**, but its authors note that quadratic attention "restricts Graphormer's application on large graphs" ([Ying et al., NeurIPS 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.05234)). GPS's own ablation is the most relevant number for us, because MalNet-Tiny is a set of function call graphs. **Removing the MPNN dropped MalNet-Tiny accuracy from 92.23% to 73.90%, while adding full attention to the MPNN raised it only to 93.50%**, and the Transformer variant doubled run time ([Rampášek et al., NeurIPS 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12454)). Tönshoff et al. showed that after tuning, GCN (0.6860 AP), GINE (0.6621) and GatedGCN match or beat GPS (0.6534) on Peptides-func. They found the depth of the prediction head "accounts for almost the complete performance gap" on Peptides-struct ([Tönshoff et al., arXiv 2309.00367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00367)).

*Where we depart.* We follow both sources: we drop global attention, and we use the same MLP head on every arm, which is Tönshoff's rule. MalNet-Tiny is Android call graphs with degree features, not x86 CFGs with block contents, and GPS criticises Graphormer-style models for using edge features only as attention bias, which our GINE messages avoid ([Rampášek et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12454)).

*Our evidence.* The comparison is mixed, and it has to be read under one selection rule. The transformer runs have only F1-selected checkpoints. Under F1 selection, **R7 scored 0.972 AUC and 0.880 macro-F1, against 0.946 ± 0.007 and 0.906 ± 0.021 for the graph transformer** (3 seeds), so the MPNN ranks better and thresholds worse (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1). The better argument is that neither structure-biased model beat its own no-structure twin (the no-graph transformer scored 0.967 / 0.916), so the operator was not the lever, which is what GPS and Tönshoff predict. The practical gain is cost and coverage: the transformer's 512-segment cap cost 0.20 macro-F1, and the MPNN keeps every block (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, decision 11).

### Three block-graph layers and two call-graph layers

*What we did.* We use 3 DirGINE layers within each function's CFG and 2 over the call graph. The plan deferred a {1, 2, 4} sweep until after the controls.

*Source and what it showed.* Oversmoothing theory says repeated normalised propagation drives node features toward a subspace that holds only connected-component and degree information. The convergence is exponential when the weight singular values are small ([Li, Han & Wu, AAAI 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606); [Oono & Suzuki, ICLR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10947)). Oversquashing is the other bound. On NeighborsMatch, **GCN and GIN failed to fit from problem radius 4** (GCN 70% training accuracy at r = 4), because they sum all neighbours before combining with the target. Adding one fully adjacent last layer cut QM9 error by 42% ([Alon & Yahav, ICLR 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05205)). Published malware hierarchies converge on 2 + 2. MalGraph found 2 GraphSAGE layers better than 1 and never tested 3 ([MalGraph](https://nesa.zju.edu.cn/download/lx_pdf_malgraph.pdf)), and Hi-GNN searched 1 to 3 layers and used 2-layer GCNs at both levels ([HiGraph](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.02113)). Flat block-graph models use 3 to 5. MAGIC used 4 DGCNN layers, NDSS 2025 used 3 GIN-0 layers, 2609.24280 used 3, and Gemini used T = 5 ([MAGIC](https://www.cs.binghamton.edu/~ghyan/papers/dsn19.pdf); [NDSS 2025](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2025-830-paper.pdf); [arXiv 2609.24280](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24280v1); [Gemini](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.06525)). GMN swept 0 to 7 propagation steps on ffmpeg CFGs, and accuracy rose with every step ([Li et al., ICML 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.12787)).

*Where we depart.* Three block layers is one more than MalGraph and Hi-GNN, and below Gemini and GMN. The oversmoothing theorems assume normalised propagation without residuals; ours has unnormalised sums and a residual path, so they do not apply directly. Its live risk is oversquashing, because sum aggregation is exactly the GIN case Alon & Yahav single out. By our inference, pooling each function to one vector before the call-graph layers plays the role of their fully adjacent layer.

*Our evidence.* **Eight CFG layers scored 0.978 AUC against 0.964 for three** (+0.014 [-0.004, +0.041]). Depth did not oversmooth, and may help (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1b). The {1, 2, 4} sweep never ran, so 3 is a precedent-based default, not a tuned value.

## Fixed readouts and the two-level hierarchy follow MalGraph, but no PE study ran our controls

### Readouts: [sum/sqrt(n), mean, max] and the "gated" attention option

*What we did.* By default, both block-to-function and function-to-binary pooling compute `GELU(Linear(3d -> d)([sum/sqrt(n) ; mean ; max]))`. The plan's pseudocode said `[sum ; mean ; max]`, but the code divides the sum by sqrt(n); `PLAN_GNN.md` now says so. The opt-in "gated" readout is `softmax_segment(w . tanh(V h))`, followed by a weighted sum. Function vectors also receive log length and a library flag, and the head receives five log-size features on every arm.

*Source and what it showed.* Xu et al. characterise the three aggregators: sum captures the full multiset, mean the distribution and max the underlying set ([Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)). PNA proves that with continuous features, discriminating multisets of size n needs at least n aggregators. That proof is the theoretical case for concatenating several readouts ([Corso et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05718)). The empirical case for fixed readouts over learned pooling is strong. **A sum-pooled bag-of-nodes baseline matched or beat DGCNN, DiffPool, ECC, GIN and GraphSAGE on D&D (78.4 vs at best 76.6), PROTEINS and ENZYMES** ([Errica et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09893)). Replacing learned cluster assignments with random or complement-graph ones "does not result in any decrease in performance" ([Mesquita et al., NeurIPS 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11418)). A 2024 benchmark found no pooling method best across datasets ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09031)). MalGraph used plain max pooling at both levels ([MalGraph](https://nesa.zju.edu.cn/download/lx_pdf_malgraph.pdf)).

*Where we depart.* **No published GNN uses sum/sqrt(n) as a graph readout; the research notes found none in two searches.** The closest precedents are message normalisations. e3nn-based models divide every sum by the square root of its number of terms ([Musaelian et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05249)), and NequIP normalises by the square root of the average neighbour count, a change credited with over 30% lower error at high temperature ([Batatia et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06643)). The defensible rationale is our inference from PNA's Theorem 2: since sqrt(n) is injective in n, sum/sqrt(n) keeps sum's size-discriminating property while growing as sqrt(n) rather than n. The "gated" name is also inaccurate (the config value stays `gated`; the docs call it attention pooling). Our score `w . tanh(V h)` with a segment softmax is Ilse et al.'s plain attention-MIL pooling (their Eqs. 7 and 8). Their gated variant multiplies in a `sigmoid(U h)` term ([Ilse et al., ICML 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04712)), and GGNN's gate is an unnormalised per-channel sigmoid sum ([Li et al., ICLR 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05493)). Ours is a weighted mean, so it is size-free. GGNN's gated sum and GMN's gated readout grow with n.

*Our evidence.* The fixed readout was enough for the no-edge hierarchy to pass the encoder gate: R2 scored **0.976 AUC and 0.924 macro-F1 against TF-IDF's 0.982 and 0.935** (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1). The gated readout never ran, because it was the gate-A fallback and gate A passed. Neither the sum/sqrt(n) term nor a mean-only size-leak check has been ablated.

### The two-level hierarchy against published systems

*What we did.* Blocks are encoded and passed through 3 CFG layers, pooled to one vector per function, passed through 2 call-graph layers, pooled to one binary vector, and classified by the shared MLP head. API references enter as block-level tokens, not as call-graph nodes.

*What the systems showed.* The table compares each published system with our design on the axes that matter here.

The pattern is consistent. Hierarchy beats flat *graph* models (MalGraph, Hi-GNN), but no system compares hierarchy against a flat *lexical* model on the same tokens. Where a structure-free or lexical control exists, it ties or wins in distribution (Ma et al., MalNet, NDSS cold start, 2609.24280). Without target-family labels, structure has paid off only under shift or in the tail: the 0.1% FPR tail on 183k samples (MalGraph), temporal drift on 682 graphs (2609.24280) and long-horizon Android drift (Hi-GNN AUT(F1) 0.715 vs GIN 0.703). With 20 to 500 target labels and domain adaptation it also paid in NDSS 2025 (graph 96.6% vs content 94.1% accuracy at 20 labels).

*Where we depart.* Our skeleton is MalGraph's with three differences: typed edges, 3 CFG layers instead of 2, and API names as block tokens instead of one-hot call-graph nodes. That last difference matters when citing MalGraph's level ablation, because its "FCG-only" arm keeps the import channel, so its hierarchy gain mixes structure with a lexical feature. Our evaluation also differs from every row. None evaluates zero-shot on families absent from training with a structure-free control on identical node features.

*Our evidence.* The hierarchy works as a bag of blocks: R2 sits within 0.006 AUC of TF-IDF. The structure inside it does not pay. In the pre-registered test with 15% block masking, **typed edges minus no edges gave +0.004 AUC [-0.001, +0.010] and +0.006 macro-F1 [-0.019, +0.026]**, so the rule failed. Typed minus shuffled passed on AUC (+0.017 [+0.001, +0.047]) but missed the macro-F1 bar (+0.010 [+0.001, +0.020]). Real wiring beats random wiring mainly because random wiring hurts (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`). This reproduces Errica's "structure not exploited" outcome on PE ransomware. Two pieces remain untested: hierarchy against a flat set of blocks (R1 was cut), and the call-graph level on its own (R7b, no call-graph message passing, never ran).

## A hand-built vocabulary without registers goes beyond every assembly model's normalisation

### Tokenization: hand-designed vocabulary vs WordPiece/BPE vs AutoTokenizer, and operand roles

*What we did.* Each instruction contributes a mnemonic token, an operand-category signature token (`sig`) and a `beh` token, and each block adds its API-reference tokens. Operands enter only as categories (register, immediate, phrase, displacement, memory, code target). Register names and operand widths never enter, because both encode the architecture (team, `docs/PLAN.md`). The block input is a sublinear-TF times IDF weighted `EmbeddingBag` over these tokens, with the IDF fitted on each run's training binaries. The vocabularies are mnemonic 609, signature 1,337 and API 14,509 in cache v2, growing to 1,321, 3,379 and 88,913 in the combined cache (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`).

*Sources and what they showed.* Learned subword vocabularies were built for open natural-language vocabularies, and their gains there were modest. BPE beat a back-off dictionary by **+0.5 BLEU (EN to DE) and +1.3 (EN to RU), and raised OOV F1 from 6.6% to 18.3%** ([Sennrich et al., ACL 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1508.07909)). GNMT's 32k wordpieces beat a word vocabulary by +1.05 BLEU (En to Fr) and +1.49 (En to De) ([Wu et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1609.08144)). Hugging Face's `tokenizers` trains such vocabularies, while `AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained` loads whatever tokenizer a checkpoint was trained with ([HF tokenizers](https://huggingface.co/docs/tokenizers/quicktour); [HF Transformers](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/fast_tokenizers)). As of October 2026, the `tokenizers` docs carry an rc0 banner saying training is not exposed in those bindings, so the installed version should be checked. Assembly models normalise to control OOV, not architecture. **PalmTree, jTrans and SAFE all keep register names as tokens** and abstract only large constants, strings and addresses ([PalmTree](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03809); [jTrans](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.12713); [SAFE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.05296)). DeepSemantic goes furthest: it abstracts general-purpose registers by size (`eax` to `reg4`), keeps stack, base and instruction pointers, and types immediates by role. With no normalisation, 97.8% of its test tokens were unseen in training. Its balanced 17,225-token scheme beat the coarse 2,022-token scheme by **F1 0.965 vs 0.946** ([Koo et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.05478)). Marcelli et al. found that a bag of the 200 most frequent block opcodes matched Gemini's hand-engineered block features, and that 1,024 opcodes added nothing ([Marcelli et al., USENIX Security 2022](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-marcelli.pdf)).

*Where we depart.* Dropping register names and widths goes beyond every published scheme; even DeepSemantic keeps size. **No paper ablates register abstraction for x86/x64 generalisation**, so the justification is our confound, not precedent. In the bundle's training split, x64 binaries were 42 ransomware and 481 goodware, while in test they were 68 ransomware and 12 goodware (team, `docs/PLAN.md`). Tokens like `rax` and `qword` would hand the model that shortcut. A hand-designed vocabulary over a closed mnemonic and signature set leaves little OOV for BPE or WordPiece to fix. The open part is API names (88,913 in the combined cache), where subword splitting could share pieces across unseen imports. That is our inference, and no source tests it.

*Our evidence.* The vocabulary is strong: TF-IDF logistic regression on signature and API tokens reaches **0.982 AUC**, the bar every deep arm has to meet. The IDF-weighted bag encoder tied the old mean-of-embeddings encoder in the no-edge hierarchy (0.976 vs 0.975 AUC) (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1). WordPiece, BPE and `AutoTokenizer` were never run, and register exclusion has no ablation; both are untested on our data.

### Frozen pretrained block encoders

*What we did.* We do not use a pretrained encoder. Block embeddings are trained end to end from the TF-IDF-weighted token bag.

*Sources and what they showed.* Maier et al. compared frozen pretrained instruction embeddings against end-to-end and random embeddings on 1.2 million Debian functions. With full labels, the averages were **PalmTree 0.86, end-to-end 0.87 and random 0.87**. At about 4,500 labels per run they were PalmTree 0.76, end-to-end 0.74 and random 0.73. The authors conclude "it is not the embedding that matters, but rather the reduced number of trainable weights", and that pre-training pays only when unlabelled data is orders of magnitude larger than labelled ([Maier et al., AsiaCCS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08682)). Marcelli et al. found that instruction embeddings "do not boost the performances of the GNN models" ([Marcelli et al.](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-marcelli.pdf)). The one PE CFG system that uses frozen PalmTree features, NDSS 2025, reports no pretrained-versus-scratch ablation ([NDSS 2025](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2025-830-paper.pdf)).

*Where we depart.* We follow Maier's first recommendation, "always try end-to-end learning". Every public assembly encoder was pretrained on Linux GCC/Clang code, and the shift to MSVC-compiled PE is unmeasured. Maier's evidence is x86 Debian functions, not malware.

*Our evidence.* No frozen-encoder arm ran. The learning curve hints that labels limit us. From 25% to 100% of training families, R7 rose from **0.945 to 0.964 AUC** while TF-IDF rose from 0.970 to 0.982 (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1b). This is the regime where Maier says pretraining can help, but only if the unlabelled corpus is in-domain and respects the family folds.

## Coarse corruption helped by about +0.02 AUC, against what the sources predicted

*What we did.* All augmentation runs in training only and is drawn per item per epoch, on the R7 model. The arms are 15% block masking (block tokens replaced by `[MASK]`, structural features zeroed, wiring intact), DropEdge at 20%, edge flipping at 20% (each surviving edge reversed with probability 0.2), token masking at 15% and 50%, uniform function dropping at 15%, and a library-biased variant (40% of library functions, 5% of the rest). The plan had ruled out edge augmentation; the edge arms ran as tests.

*Sources and what they showed.* The 15% rate has two independent sources. BERT selects 15% of tokens, replacing them 80/10/10 with `[MASK]`, a random token or the original. Its ablation of those mixes spans only **83.6 to 84.4 MNLI** ([Devlin et al., NAACL 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.04805)). Hu et al.'s attribute masking hides 15% of node or edge attributes ([Hu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12265)). Both are pretraining objectives, not supervised augmentations. The one malware study blanked Android opcodes at rates from 0.05 to 0.5. The best rate was **alpha = 0.2, which raised F1 from 0.9494 to 0.9554**, and every rate up to 0.5 stayed above baseline ([McLaughlin & Martinez del Rincon](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.11821)). No source sets 50%; GraphMAE's 0.25 to 0.75 mask rates are the nearest ([GraphMAE, KDD 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10803)). DropEdge is an anti-oversmoothing device for deep node classifiers. On Cora it raised an 8-layer GCN from 78.70 to 85.80 but a 2-layer one only from 86.10 to 86.50 ([Rong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10903)). Edge flipping is GraphCL's edge perturbation, "randomly adding or dropping" a 0.2 ratio of edges. It **hurt NCI1 molecules "regardless of augmentation strength"**, while node dropping and subgraph sampling were "generally beneficial" ([You et al., NeurIPS 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.13902)). Under covariate shift, **DropEdge fell to 45.08 against 68.66 for ERM on Motif-base**, while masking only the environment part beat ERM ([Sui et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.02843v2)). DropEdge-style dropping also hurts long-range tasks ([Singh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07364v1)). No source covers function dropping; GraphCL's node dropping is the nearest analogue.

*Where we depart.* GraphCL's rates size contrastive views, not supervised augmentation. Our edge flip reverses edges instead of adding random ones. The 50% token rate and function dropping have no primary source. The library-biased arm is our transfer of AIA's environment-masking prior to FLIRT and thunk functions.

*Our evidence.* Seed 0, change in AUC against R7 (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, day 1b):

| Arm | AUC / macro-F1 | dAUC vs R7 [95% CI] | Literature prediction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Block masking 15% | 0.986 / 0.935 | **+0.022 [+0.005, +0.054]** | Small gain (Hu, BERT rate) |
| Edge flip 20% | 0.986 / 0.888 | **+0.022 [+0.003, +0.050]** | Harm (GraphCL, NCI1) |
| DropEdge 20% | 0.982 / 0.908 | **+0.018 [+0.004, +0.038]** | Harm under shift (AIA); small gain at shallow depth (Rong) |
| Function dropping 15% | 0.972 / 0.921 | +0.007 [-0.018, +0.041] | No direct source |
| Token masking 50% | 0.963 / 0.915 | -0.001 [-0.014, +0.009] | No source; nearest says no collapse |
| Token masking 15% | 0.957 / 0.910 | -0.007 [-0.048, +0.016] | Under 1 F1 point (McLaughlin) |
| Library dropping 40% (+5% others) | 0.944 / 0.921 | -0.020 [-0.107, +0.039] | Gain (AIA environment masking) |

Block masking is the only arm with a three-seed follow-up. It brought the GNN to TF-IDF level (paired dAUC +0.004 [-0.004, +0.015]). Token masking matched the opcode result: no measurable gain. **The predicted harm from DropEdge and edge flipping did not appear.** The edge-corrupting arms helped as much as block masking, so the plan reads all three as regularisation of an overfitting model, not as evidence that wiring matters. AIA's harm was measured on synthetic motif graphs whose label lives in the structure. Here the label lives mostly in block contents, so corrupting edges costs little and regularises a lot. That is our reading, consistent with the null structure result. Every arm except block masking is seed 0 only.

## The evaluation design caught a false seed-0 structure signal

### Family-disjoint 5-fold holdout

*What we did.* Test families never appear in training, and goodware is grouped by identity. The validation carve takes whole families. The primary protocol is x86 k-fold with 3 seeds x 5 folds and pooled out-of-fold predictions.

*Sources and what they showed.* Geirhos et al. define shortcuts as "decision rules that perform well on i.i.d. test data but fail on o.o.d. tests", and argue that o.o.d. tests must "become the rule rather than the exception" ([Geirhos et al., Nature MI 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.07780)). TESSERACT names spatial and temporal bias. Under realistic constraints, AUT(F1, 24 months) was **0.58 for DREBIN and 0.32 for MaMaDroid against published F1 up to 0.99** ([Pendlebury et al., USENIX Security 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1807.07838)). Dambra et al. tested Windows detectors on 1.5k families excluded from training and reached **0.815 accuracy with static features** ([Dambra et al., CCS 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.14657)). NDSS 2025 holds out one family at a time but adapts with 20 to 500 target labels ([NDSS 2025](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2025-830-paper.pdf)).

*Where we depart.* Our split is not temporal, so TESSERACT's constraint C1 (training strictly before testing) is unmet. Family labels are noisy, and related families can straddle folds, so family holdout bounds leakage from below rather than closing it. No PE GNN paper evaluates zero-shot on unseen families with a structure-free control. Ma et al.'s drift split is within-family, NDSS uses target labels, and 2609.24280 has no family labels.

*Our evidence.* The split is what turns R2-versus-R7 into a real test. It also leaves 712 clusters among 1,810 binaries, which sets the effective sample size for every interval in this document.

### Architecture confound, class x architecture reweighting and cell-balanced metrics

*What we did.* The primary results are x86-only. For the all-architecture v3 evaluation, `--arch-balance` weights each training sample by `N / (C x n_cell)` over (label, architecture) cells, replacing the class weight. Metrics add a cell-balanced block in which each class x architecture cell carries equal weight.

*Sources and what they showed.* Arp et al. list spurious correlations as pitfall P4. In their study of 30 top-venue papers, every paper had at least three pitfalls, and removing one spurious artifact from an authorship-attribution dataset cut accuracy by 48% ([Arp et al., USENIX Security 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.09470)). Sagawa et al. define groups as (spurious attribute, label) pairs. Group DRO raised **Waterbirds worst-group accuracy from 6.7 to 86.0** ([Sagawa et al., ICLR 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.08731)). Idrissi et al. show that plain group reweighting nearly matches it. RWG scored **87.6 on Waterbirds and 72.0 on CivilComments, against ERM's 85.5 and 61.3**, and model selection by average rather than worst-group validation accuracy cost 10.4 to 17.2 points ([Idrissi et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.14503)).

*Where we depart.* No malware paper we found treats CPU architecture as a confounder. Our reweighting is RWG with groups = class x architecture, imported from vision and NLP. Idrissi's main lesson is that *selection* must be group-aware, and the plan adds cell-balanced metrics to reports but does not state that checkpoint and threshold selection on v3 are cell-balanced. That is a gap to close before the GNN `-ab` arms run.

*Our evidence.* The confound is large. On the v3 all-architecture pool (ransomware 2,421 x86 / 741 x64, goodware 968 / 1,522), **a rule that predicts ransomware whenever a binary is x86 scores 0.690 macro-F1**, and the cell-balanced metric takes it to exactly 0.500. For TF-IDF, arch-balancing raised cell-balanced AUC from 0.960 to 0.967 and cell-balanced macro-F1 at 0.5 from 0.902 to 0.909, while ordinary AUC fell from 0.974 to 0.969. That is the expected trade: the model stops being paid for architecture (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, step 7). The GNN `-ab` arms are queued, not run.

### Validation-chosen threshold

*What we did.* Deep arms pick the threshold on the family-grouped validation carve, with dual checkpoints (AUC-selected and F1-selected). TF-IDF uses inner family-grouped CV thresholds.

*Sources and what they showed.* Arp et al.'s pitfall P5, biased parameter selection, covers thresholds tuned on test data. Data snooping appears in about 73% of the papers they reviewed ([Arp et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.09470)). Idrissi's selection result above is the group-aware version of the same rule.

*Where we depart.* The deep arms use one small carve instead of inner CV, to save GPU time (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`, decision 16).

*Our evidence.* Honest thresholds cost points, and the checkpoint rule reorders arms. On v3, TF-IDF macro-F1 was **0.917 at 0.5 but 0.885 at the validation threshold**. The no-edge GNN passed gate A under AUC selection (0.976) but not under F1 selection (0.926), because F1 checkpoints landed on epochs 1 to 4 (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`). This is why AUC and PR-AUC lead every gate.

### Paired cluster bootstrap and a pre-registered effect size

*What we did.* `stats.py` resamples clusters (ransomware families and single goodware binaries) 2,000 times, applies each draw to all seeds, and reports paired differences. The pre-registered rule, fixed before stage B, requires **dAUC >= 0.01 and dmacro-F1 >= 0.02, pooled, with CIs excluding zero, over 3 seeds x 5 folds**.

*Sources and what they showed.* The cluster bootstrap gives consistent variance estimates under both the transformation and random-effect models ([Field & Welsh, JRSS-B 2007](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssb/v69y2007i3p369-390.html)). Miller shows clustered standard errors **up to 3x naive (1.34 vs 0.44 on DROP)**, and that pairing cuts variance by a third at a between-model correlation of 0.5 ([Miller, arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)). Bouthillier et al. find data sampling the largest source of benchmark variance. In their simulations, **single-run comparisons gave about 10% false positives and 75% false negatives**, and they recommend P(A > B) above about 0.75 as the decision rule ([Bouthillier et al., MLSys 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03098)). Pre-registration in ML has run as NeurIPS 2020 and 2021 workshops ([NeurIPS 2020 workshop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2020/public/workshop_16158.html)). In malware ML, 2609.24280 used a paired bootstrap, but over 223 test graphs rather than clusters ([arXiv 2609.24280](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24280v1)).

*Where we depart.* We found no malware-ML paper that clusters the bootstrap by family or pre-registers an effect size, so both are imports from statistics and general ML. We use thresholds on mean differences with CIs, not Bouthillier's P(A > B), and ran no power analysis of the kind Miller recommends.

*Our evidence.* The rule did what Bouthillier's numbers say it should. With 15% block masking at seed 0, **typed minus no edges was +0.009 [+0.001, +0.019] and typed minus shuffled +0.012 [+0.004, +0.021]**, both above zero. At 3 seeds the first became +0.004 [-0.001, +0.010], and the second passed on AUC but missed the macro-F1 bar (team, `docs/PLAN_GNN.md`). A single-seed claim would have been a false positive. The 3-seed intervals also show where the rule is underpowered (our arithmetic). The AUC interval is about 0.011 wide, enough to detect a 0.01 effect. The macro-F1 interval for typed minus no edges is 0.045 wide, more than twice the 0.02 bar, so the macro-F1 half of the rule cannot reliably confirm an effect of that size with 712 clusters.

## Conclusion

The literature review changes what the design document should claim. The layer (sum aggregation, edge-type embeddings, directional split, pre-norm residual) is a sound, well-sourced message-passing operator, but its sources justify expressiveness and trainability, not a prediction that CFG wiring carries ransomware signal. The sources that bear on that prediction (Errica, Mesquita, MalNet, Ma et al., NDSS cold start, 2609.24280 in distribution) all predicted the null we measured. Our result therefore extends a known pattern to a setting nobody had tested: family-disjoint PE ransomware, with a shuffle control and a lexical baseline. It does not contradict the GNN literature. The two failed predictions are more informative than the confirmations. Edge corruption and depth both helped or did no harm, which fits a model whose label lives in block contents and whose edges act mainly as a regularisation surface.

What remains is narrower than it looks. The departures without precedent (sum/sqrt(n), register exclusion, typed CFG edges, learnable eps with last-layer readout) are each one ablation away from being either supported or dropped. The untested choices that could still change the verdict are the call-graph level alone (R7b), stacking on TF-IDF (R8), and the arch-balanced GNN arms, where MalGraph's and 2609.24280's evidence says structure is most likely to show: under shift and in robustness, not in pooled in-distribution AUC. The macro-F1 half of the pre-registered rule needs either more clusters or a looser bar to be informative.
